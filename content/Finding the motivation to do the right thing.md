I grew up with a general high school education, in a sort of "master of none and a jack of no trades" way. To contribute meaningfully to any particular area of progress, I would need either specialized education or enough personal interest to teach myself.

I'm pretty sympathetic to the idea of finding a university subject and just attending evening lectures, but so far I haven't found a subject I would like to invest that much effort in (most likely because I haven't looked hard enough). So what I'm left with is my own drive to learn.

The one thing I learned from my years at school is how to learn. What's missing is the motivation to spend hours researching something for no other reward than the elusive "making the world a better place".

I think our motivation is limited, so we need to decide where to spend it. Two things can help us make the most of it: genuine interest, which makes effort easier, and moral or religious principles, which can push us toward important work we would not otherwise choose.

# Genuine interest

Let's start with the former. If there was a way to improve the world by grinding hundreds of hours in a single videogame, we would already be living in a utopia. But sometimes the stars do align.

For me personally, I believe [[our future is in space]]. I think rockets are cool and if I had the option to go back a few years, pick an engineering school and move my entire life to Florida/Texas, That's what I would be working on.

My other strong belief is that we should be (and are) moving towards a single [[world government]] and any way we can speed up the transition is very important to me. However I find politics to be appalling and get no enjoyment from convincing uninterested people or building channels for international cooperation. That's where the other source of motivation comes in.

# Moral/religious imperative

It doesn't matter if your principles come from religion or you make them up yourself, but we should take seriously the consequences of acting against them, especially when our actions affect others.

If you believe you’re entitled to peace and quiet at night but regularly roar through other people’s neighborhoods on a motorcycle at 11 p.m., what kind of a world are you helping to create?

Oftentimes the principles we hold lead us to areas we are not that comfortable with. If I want to live in a world where animals stop being cruelly abused, I should switch to a vegan diet. Why don't I do it then? I should have the motivation required if adhering to my morals comes before anything else.

The issue is that moral demands compete with one another for the same limited supply of time, attention and willpower. Becoming vegan would require some sacrifice from me, while the effort spent making that change is effort I cannot spend elsewhere. I still consider eating meat harmful, but I have to decide where my limited capacity for deliberate change can do the most good.

I allow myself some comforts, because trying to optimize every part of my life would quickly exhaust the motivation I have. I would rather concentrate that motivation on the areas where my abilities can have the greatest effect.

# Moving from reason to results

In this way, we have reasoned our way into knowing what the good thing to do is. What remains is the harder part: coercing the resting body and mind into doing the worst thing imaginable — _**effort**_.

This is the part I struggle with immensely, so my advice may not be universally applicable. What has worked for me is setting aside a reasonably small amount of time each day, enough to make visible progress over some time, but not so much that the effort becomes exhausting and I start feeling that I am wasting my limited free time on something that isn't going anywhere.

By spending just 30 minutes every day taking care of this note vault and blog, I slowly but surely add value to it while still leaving myself enough time to rest after work. Hopefully, after doing this for long enough, managing my notes will become almost automatic. From there, I can gradually increase the amount of effort spent on harder things — projects where interest and principle tell me my time is actually worth spending.
